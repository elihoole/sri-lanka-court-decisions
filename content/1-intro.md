# Introduction {#sec:intro}

In common-law jurisdictions, judicial precedent – generally understood as appellate rulings that bind lower courts on questions of law – determines the authoritative interpretation of the law. As such, precedent occupies a central place in judicial, academic and professional work. In practice, however, it is difficult to assess how precedent is identified or applied by courts. Computational methods, broadly described as the use of data science and artificial intelligence to identify patterns across large datasets, offer a way to move beyond this narrow focus by enabling systematic analysis of the full corpus of case law. These methods enable analysis of large corpora (in this case, all Supreme Court and Court of Appeal judgments published online for the 11-year window from January 1, 2012 to December 31, 2022) that would be impractical to undertake manually. In doing so, they generate new forms of knowledge that extend beyond jurisprudence driven by landmark rulings. Such knowledge has the potential to unsettle received wisdom and ingrained perceptions about the working of courts. Put differently, computational analysis can make visible dimensions of judicial activity that approaches focused on landmark decisions often obscure.

This article is a first attempt to bring such empirical analysis into the doctrinal and normative study of Sri Lanka’s superior courts. We apply computational methods to analyse judgments of the Supreme Court and Court of Appeal from 2012 to 2022, drawing on a corpus comprising judgments made available online by each court. Using exploratory data analysis, mutual information analysis, and topic modelling, we address three questions. First, what new insights can we obtain when we go beyond the landmark judgment approach (and paradigm) in studying the substance and volume of appellate work? Second, what can patterns in judgments reveal about the relative institutional significance of each court, and about the effects of constitutional change on judicial independence? Third, what do these patterns reveal about access to justice before Sri Lanka’s superior courts?

The findings demonstrate that the Court of Appeal’s numerical dominance far exceeds its doctrinal recognition; that writ petitions vastly outnumber fundamental rights petitions yet remain understudied, with the Court of Appeal serving as the effective final forum in the overwhelming majority of writ cases; that the constitutional time limit is not adhered to in the determination of fundamental rights petitions while writ petitions before the Court of Appeal are determined in a more timely manner; that land disputes and state employment matters dominate appellate dockets; and that linguistic patterns and decision length differ between the two courts. These patterns, we think, have significant implications for access to justice, legal scholarship, and institutional reform.
