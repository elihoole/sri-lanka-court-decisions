# Insights for Administration of Justice, Legal Scholarship and Reform {#sec:insights}

In this section we briefly analyse the implications of the patterns that we have identified in @sec:analysis. We have demonstrated thus far, the way in which computational methods make visible patterns obscured by the landmark case or precedent-focused approaches that dominate legal scholarship and professional discourse on case law, with `SriLankaCourtDecisions` enabling systematic study of appellate jurisprudence that was previously impossible. By analysing the decisions published over a decade rather than selecting landmark cases, this approach reveals what courts actually do—not just what they say in celebrated constitutional moments. These insights can be refined further by comparing and contrasting them with a similar study of writ matters before the Provincial High Courts, work that we leave for another day.

Topic modelling identifies the substantive areas that dominate dockets. Mutual information analysis reveals linguistic patterns that encode institutional hierarchies. Quantitative analysis of decision volumes, timing, length, and outcomes provides empirical grounding for claims about judicial work, independence, and access to justice. These methods do not replace doctrinal analysis but rather, complement it by revealing the empirical realities within which doctrine operates, including by identifying gaps between doctrine, norms and practice. The patterns identified here—quantitative dominance, temporal dysfunction, substantive concentration—are not speculative. They are measurable features of appellate jurisprudence that demand explanation and, where warranted, reform. Insights from `SriLankaCourtDecisions` do not resolve these questions, but it provides an empirical foundation for engaging them.

The findings we have presented in @sec:analysis are preliminary but sufficient to challenge received assumptions and warrant attention in the administration of justice, legal scholarship as well as in reform efforts. We present a research agenda that can flow from this work at the end of this article.

## Quantitative Significance of the Court of Appeal

In comparison to the Supreme Court, in relation to the number of judgments issued, the Court of Appeal numerically dominates. The Court of Appeal issues nearly six times as many judgments as the Supreme Court—yet remains jurisprudentially neglected. This quantitative contribution is understood only in an ad hoc manner by judiciary, practitioners, academics, and court observers. The doctrinal weight afforded to these two jurisdictions inverts their quantitative relationship and does not shed adequate light on their functional separation.

Why does the Court of Appeal, handling a larger volume of cases, resolve writs faster than the Supreme Court resolves fundamental rights petitions? This comparative puzzle raises questions about structural causes: backlog, judicial resources, and procedural practices and professional culture. The answer may lie in a combination of factors including differences in case complexity, the nature of public law adjudication, and institutional practices—but these require further investigation.

During the period under review, the number of judgments on fundamental rights by the Supreme Court increased at the same time as the enactment of the 19th Amendment. In contrast, 2013–2015, a period marked by institutional crisis, saw depressed output. The Court of Appeal's output, by contrast, peaked in 2013. These patterns can be studied further to examine how and why litigants and practitioners chose between the two courts as well as to identify whether there can be a correlation between institutional stability, litigant/lawyer confidence in courts with the number of judgments issued.

Through topic modelling we identified land disputes and state employment disputes as dominant themes in the public law jurisprudence of these courts. If delays and heavy caseloads are to be dealt with, reform efforts must address these areas specifically. Substantive concentration in land disputes and state employment cases reveals that superior courts function primarily as forums for resolving disputes with the state or its instrumentalities of the everyday type, rather than of significant issues of law that ought to warrant the attention of superior courts. This pattern raises questions about the accessibility and effectiveness of lower courts for resolving such disputes and the role of superior courts in managing state-citizen relations. Whether this concentration represents institutional design, litigation incentives, or systemic dysfunction warrants further investigation.

## The Question of Time

In the absence of even a directive regarding timely conclusion, writ petitions in the Court of Appeal are determined sooner than petitions on fundamental rights. In both courts however some petitions take much longer. These patterns provide an empirical basis for the concerns raised about delay but also shed light on time as a relative factor between the two courts. The inversion between the two courts suggests that formal rules matter less than institutional capacity, resource allocation, and procedural culture. The structural causes remain unknown and demand investigation.

Systematic delays undermine the rule of law regardless of ultimate outcomes. The gap between procedural promise and empirical reality imposes costs on litigants—costs that fall disproportionately on those with fewer resources to sustain protracted litigation. Addressing delays requires confronting institutional capacity, case management practices, and procedural realities that quantitative analysis makes visible.

## Institutional Hierarchies and Access to Justice

Mutual-information and decision-length analyses identify differences between the courts in vocabulary, textual form, and volume. Supreme Court judgments more frequently employ the language of error and questions of law, while Supreme Court Fundamental Rights judgments are generally longer than Court of Appeal writ judgments. These patterns are consistent with differences in institutional role. Inferences on doctrinal hierarchy or depth of reasoning require qualitative analysis.

From an access to justice perspective, our assessment reveals that in effect, the Court of Appeal is the final arbiter, the primary court for judicial review in Sri Lanka. The Court of Appeal provides regular, accessible, and functionally final review of administrative action and jurisdictional errors. The low ratio of Supreme Court appeals suggests that Court of Appeal writ remedies effectively resolve disputes in most cases, making this jurisdiction a more substantial avenue for access to justice than its current recognition acknowledges.

## Implications for Reform

The findings of this study point toward specific areas for institutional reform, but implementing such reforms requires additional empirical work. If land disputes dominate long-duration cases, would specialised land tribunals reduce the burden on superior courts? If state employment disputes constitute a substantial proportion of writ petitions, would strengthening the Administrative Appeals Tribunal and other such mechanisms reduce litigation?

Piloting procedural reforms – such as time limits for oral submissions, case management protocols for particular dispute types, or alternative dispute resolution mechanisms for specific categories of cases – would benefit from empirical baseline data against which to measure effectiveness. The `SriLankaCourtDecisions` corpus provides such baseline data for the superior courts, but implementing and evaluating reform requires collaboration between researchers, judiciary, legal practitioners, and policymakers.

The constitutional mandate for two-month determination of fundamental rights petitions cannot be met under current conditions, but meaningful reform requires understanding what is feasible. Are there categories of fundamental rights petitions that could be resolved more quickly? Do particular procedural stages consume disproportionate time? Could interim relief mechanisms provide meaningful remedies while final adjudication proceeds?

The limitations of this study reflect broader limitations in Sri Lanka's legal system digitisation. At the time we began to work on the research for this paper, court registries were not digitised, case management systems were incomplete, and no centralised repository of judicial decisions existed. In 2026, many of these gaps are being addressed. The recent efforts to digitise the administration of justice bodes well for empirical legal research.

Building infrastructure for empirical legal research requires institutional investment in digitising records, standardising case numbering, and making data publicly accessible. Such infrastructure would benefit not only researchers but also legal practitioners, policymakers, and the public. Searchable, systematically organised court decisions would improve access to justice by making legal knowledge more readily available. Digitised registries would enable better case management and reduce administrative burden on court staff. Publicly accessible data on case filings, dispositions, and timelines would enhance transparency and accountability.

We propose that completing the digitisation of decisions from all levels of the judicial hierarchy and making them accessible through stable, searchable platforms be a priority. The `SriLankaCourtDecisions` corpus demonstrates what is possible with publicly available data, but the limitations of the dataset reflect underlying gaps in legal system infrastructure. Addressing those gaps would enable far more comprehensive empirical analysis and, more importantly, would contribute to a more transparent, accessible, and accountable justice system.
