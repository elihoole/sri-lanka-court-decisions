# Conclusion {#sec:conclusion}

Computational analysis of the \texttt{SriLankaCourtDecisions} corpus—comprising 1,278 Supreme Court and 7,314 Court of Appeal decisions from 2012–2022—reveals patterns that challenge received assumptions about Sri Lanka's appellate courts. Three findings stand out. First, the Court of Appeal's numerical dominance (nearly six times the Supreme Court's output) inverts its doctrinal recognition; writ petitions vastly outnumber fundamental rights petitions, yet receive far less scholarly attention. The low ratio of Supreme Court appeals to Court of Appeal writ decisions (31:1,741) confirms that the Court of Appeal functions as the effective final arbiter for most litigants seeking judicial review. Second, systematic delays characterise both jurisdictions: the Supreme Court averages 4.17 years to resolve fundamental rights petitions—rendering the constitutional two-month mandate meaningless—while the Court of Appeal, without any such mandate, resolves writ petitions faster (mean 3.15 years). Third, land disputes and state employment matters dominate appellate dockets, raising questions about whether current reform efforts targeting commercial disputes address the cases that actually burden the courts.

These patterns have implications for access to justice, institutional design, and legal scholarship. For most citizens seeking relief against administrative action, the Court of Appeal—not the Supreme Court—is the practical forum for justice. The delays documented here impose real costs on litigants, costs that fall disproportionately on those with fewer resources to sustain protracted litigation. The concentration of cases in land and state employment disputes suggests that appellate courts function primarily as forums for resolving routine state-citizen disputes rather than the constitutional questions that dominate scholarly discourse.

The \texttt{SriLankaCourtDecisions} corpus, which we make publicly available, enables verification and extension of these findings. The limitations of this study—incomplete temporal data, absence of leave-stage dismissals, gaps in digitisation—define priorities for future empirical work. What computational methods make visible, however, is already sufficient to warrant attention in both scholarly analysis and reform debates.
