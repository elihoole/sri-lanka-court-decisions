# Abstract {-}

**Key Insights:**

The \texttt{SriLankaCourtDecisions} corpus of judgments on writs and fundamental rights, delivered from 2012 to 2022, reveals that:

1. The Court of Appeal delivers nearly six times more judgments than the Supreme Court annually, yet its jurisprudence remains systematically understudied.
2. Numerically, judgments on writ petitions vastly outnumber judgments on fundamental rights petitions.
3. During the study period, the Supreme Court decided only 31 appeals from Court of Appeal writ decisions, compared to 1,741 writ decisions issued by the Court of Appeal. While individual cases cannot be tracked through the appellate system, this low ratio (an implied appeal rate of 1.8%) suggests that at a practical level the Court of Appeal functions effectively as the first and final arbiter of writ petitions.
4. The Supreme Court takes an average of 4.17 years to resolve fundamental rights petitions, and the Court of Appeal resolves writ petitions faster (mean 3.15 years).
5. Land disputes and state employment matters are the dominant themes in appellate jurisprudence.
6. The linguistic patterns and decision length in these judgments confirm, empirically, doctrinal hierarchies. Judgments by the Supreme Court are lengthier and more complex in terms of linguistic patterns.
7. At a surface level, institutional stability and number of judgments reveal a relationship that requires further investigation within the Supreme Court as well as when comparing the Supreme Court with the Court of Appeal.

**Keywords:** empirical legal research; computational methods; Sri Lanka; Supreme Court; Court of Appeal; fundamental rights jurisdiction; writ jurisdiction; access to justice.
