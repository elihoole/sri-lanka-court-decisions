# Empirical Study of Sri Lankan Courts**

Legal empiricism has been defined as research that includes 'systematic, observational study of law, whether quantitative or qualitative, contemporary or historical, direct or archival'.[@suchman2010legal, p. 563] Within this broad area of legal research, the study of 'judicial behaviour' is in its early days.[@cane2010oxford, p. 1] Data on court cases has been described as 'judicial statistics' and early efforts at reporting on such statistics is dated back to 1872, in the United States.[@kritzer2010forgotten, p. 877] An early example of research on judicial statistics is *The Business of the Supreme Court: A Study in the Federal Judicial System*.[@frankfurter1928business; @kritzer2010forgotten, p. 891] This type of research too has been described as the study of judicial behaviour and broadly covers judicial personalities, judicial attitudes and case load.[^note_judicial_studies] Realists took the view that 'judgments were influenced by personal and social factors, and ought therefore to be analysed not only with reference to statute, precedent, and established legal principle, but also with reference to judges' social backgrounds and political beliefs.'[@baldwin2010empirical, p. 882]

Empirical legal research confronts methodological constraints: unlike in natural sciences, we cannot establish causal inference from observational data in legal studies.[@epstein2014introduction, pp. 6-7] What we can offer are descriptive statistical inferences and a publicly available dataset for verification and further study.[@epstein2014introduction, pp. 11-12, 15] A point of comparison may be the US Supreme Court Database.[^note_scdb] This database is supported by the American National Science Foundation. It contains the cases decided by the US Supreme Court 1791 – 2022 and offers over 'two hundred pieces of information about each case' including the votes of the justices.[@scdb2024] Another point of reference is the Concourtstats of South Africa, a project which provides empirical analysis of the jurisprudence and work of the Constitutional Court of South Africa.[@concourtstats] One of the aims of this type of an empirical approach is to test received wisdom on theories about the work of the appellate courts.[^note_legal_theory] This article attempts to do so in the Sri Lankan context.

The study of jurisprudence of the Sri Lankan apex courts has rarely been supplemented with quantitative analysis. Kishali Pinto Jayawardene's work on the writ of habeas corpus and the work of one of our authors with regard to fundamental rights judgments are two early examples.[@pintojayawardena2011habeas; @samararatne2016recent; @samararatne2023judicial] In her work, Jayawardene offers a quantitative and qualitative analysis of 844 habeas corpus writ petitions. Jayawardene's work established that the habeas corpus writ is ineffective in Sri Lanka, despite the influential jurisprudence in this jurisdiction such as the *Leeda Violet* case and the *Plantain Point* camp case.[^note_habeas_cases] In *Leeda Violet*, the Court of Appeal held that where a person has been taken into custody and can no longer be found, the state will be held responsible and ordered that compensation be paid. A similar approach was followed in the *Plantain Point* case. These two cases represent a significant development of jurisprudence on the habeas corpus writ. However, Jayawardene's empirical study finds that out of 844 petitions examined, 676 were dismissed, in 163 the persons detained were released and the writ was issued in 19.[@pintojayawardena2011habeas, p. 87 ff] These findings raise significant questions about the weightage we attach to jurisprudence and judicial precedent, and how we should account for the regular decision-making of courts. We return to these questions later in this article. The empirical approach established by Jayawardene on habeas corpus petitions—examining quantitative patterns alongside doctrinal significance—informs our broader analysis of writ jurisdiction across the Court of Appeal's Article 140 docket and that of the Supreme Court under Article 126.

A more recent example of empirical analysis is the work by Sandani Abeyawardene on topic modelling of judgments of two supreme courts: that of Sri Lanka (2013-2020) and the United Kingdom (2009 – 2022).[@abeywardena2023reading] Abeyawardene uses Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) with Machine Learning for Language Toolkit (MALLET) to identify the most significant topics in the jurisprudence of each jurisdiction. Beyond these contributions, empirical legal research in Sri Lanka has been rare.[@suchman2010legal, p. 563] Neelan Tiruchelvam's work on mediation boards is perhaps an early example of empirical legal work.[@tiruchelvam1984ideology] Tiruchelvam's work is also identified as an example of the law and society approach to the law in Sri Lanka. More recent examples of this approach would be one of our author's work on pioneering women in Sri Lanka's legal profession and on Sri Lanka's first Election Commission.[@samararatne2021election]

In relation to fundamental rights jurisprudence, through a simple descriptive analysis of the type of fundamental right most commonly invoked in judgments on fundamental rights since 2006, one of our authors has established two key findings. One is that the most commonly invoked fundamental right is Art 12, that is the right to equality. Of the 303 judgements issued 2010 to 2020, 258 invoked the right to equality.[@samararatne2023judicial, p. 14] The second is that this Article is overwhelmingly invoked by public servants to challenge recruitments, transfers or promotions by the state as an employer.[@samararatne2016recent, p. 238] Here too, there is an interesting contrast between jurisprudence and judicial precedent as interpreted through the conventional approach to law and the empirical examination of cases. Jurisprudentially, significant developments have been achieved by the Sri Lankan judiciary with regard to several fundamental rights. For instance, courts have recognized that the right to be free from torture includes the right to be free from sexual violence.[^note_torture_case] Courts have recognized new rights, such as the rights of persons with disabilities and rights to the environment.[^note_new_rights_cases] The distinction between fundamental rights jurisdiction and writ jurisdiction—often treated as blending in scholarly discourse—is confirmed empirically by differences in volume, forum, timing, and outcomes, as demonstrated in Section 4.

The analysis offered here is exploratory and descriptive, not normative. The triangulation of quantitative analysis with qualitative analysis remains for future research. For a fuller discussion of the methodological and data-related constraints affecting this study, see section 4.

**1.1	The Development of the Legal System**

Sri Lanka's modern legal system has been shaped by its encounters with colonialism. Today it is one of the three mixed legal systems in the world.[^note_mixed_systems] Between 1505 CE to 1948 CE, a long period of 443 years, Sri Lanka was under partial colonial rule of the Portuguese, the Dutch, and, finally, under the complete control of the British. In 1972, it became a republic. During this time, the colonisers established court systems, introduced their laws and established legal institutions.[@rupesinghe2024lawmaking] The Portuguese had the least amount of territorial as well as political control whereas the Dutch had significant control over Sri Lanka's maritime provinces.[@cooray1992introduction, p. 1 ff] The British, unlike the other two colonial powers, gained and maintained total control over the whole island for over 130 years. Sri Lanka was a crown colony since 1802. Sri Lanka's Supreme Court, established in 1801 is known to be one the oldest in the region and Sri Lanka was the first crown colony to have a jury system.[^note_charters]

Due to the establishment of a court system by the Dutch, the Roman-Dutch law was established as Sri Lanka's common law. Today, the Roman-Dutch law applies in relation to property and persons while English common law is recognized as the law that may be used to fill in lacunae in legislation. During its long colonial rule, the British laid a firm foundation for Sri Lanka's legal system. The Dutch recognized, codified and applied what was described as 'personal laws' or laws/customs of distinct ethnic/religious communities in the Island; the Tesawalamai, Muslim law and the Kandyan law.[^note_customary_laws] In keeping with their general policies, the British continued to recognize these laws and the Roman-Dutch law that had been introduced by the Dutch. Selectively and systematically, the British introduced legislation and English common law during their rule. This resulted in a complex and mixed legal system that continues to remain in place. Sri Lanka declared itself a Republic in 1972 and has since then had two constitutions. The current Constitution, enacted in 1978 has been amended 21 times.

In this context, the law in Sri Lanka is developed through multiple avenues: constitutional reform, legislation, case law, regulations and sometimes even through policies. Case law is the primary means of developing the law in several areas including constitutional law, administrative law, criminal law, land law, family law and the law of damages. In the case of public law, judicial precedent determines how the relevant legal basis (constitution, legislation or the common law) is applied. In the case of land law and family law, legislation applies along with the Roman Dutch law. The law of damages is governed primarily by the Roman-Dutch law. Case law is central to the development of the law both in the substantive and procedural sense in Sri Lanka. The Supreme Court is the highest court with the exclusive mandate to interpret the Constitution and decisions of this Court bind all courts on all aspects of law.[^note_art118]

Delays in the Sri Lanka court system are extensive. In 2017, a Parliamentary Select Committee found that it takes an average of 17 years for a criminal case to be concluded.[@parliament2017criminal, p. 4] The lower courts are under the authority of Sri Lanka's Judicial Service Commission (JSC). In its annual report, the JSC records the number of cases filed, concluded etc. However, similar reports are not available for the Court of Appeal and Supreme Court on an annual basis.

**1.2	Superior Courts and Access to Judgments**

**1.2.1	Publication and Reporting History**

Judgments of the Supreme Court and the Court of Appeal from the years 1809 to 1978 are recorded in volumes titled the 'Ceylon New Law Reports'. Although Sri Lanka gained independence from Britain in 1948, it remained under the Dominion of the British Crown and was known as the Dominion of Ceylon. It was only in 1972 upon the adoption of an autochthonous Republican Constitution, that Sri Lanka gained complete independence from the British Crown and became the Democratic Socialist Republic of Sri Lanka. Starting from 1978, upon the adoption of the Second Republican Constitution, select judgments of the Supreme Court were published in volumes titled the 'Sri Lanka Law Reports'. The judgments that would be reported in this way were selected by a committee of Justices of the Court, headed by the Chief Justice. Physical copies of judgments recorded in both the Ceylon New Law Reports and the Sri Lanka Law Reports are available at the National Library in Colombo, and digital copies are available on the website run by the Ministry of Justice (<https://www.lawnet.gov.lk>).

**1.2.2	Digitization and Contemporary Access**

Since 2012, all judgments of both apex courts are also available as digital copies, accessible on the official government websites of the Supreme Court (<https://www.supremecourt.lk>) and the Court of Appeal (<https://www.courtofappeal.lk>).[^note_ssl_invalid] This led to a paradigm shift in terms of access to the jurisprudence of the apex courts. Prior to this, accessing judgments presented practical challenges. Until a case was reported, access to the judgment could only be through a party to the case or through a formal application to the relevant court registry along with the payment of the required fees. Unreported judgments were not known or accessible except through personal knowledge of such case or through media reports.

The official government websites for both the Supreme Court and the Court of Appeal have published judgments made by the apex courts from 2009 onwards. The Sri Lanka Law Reports continue to be published and the judgments reported in those volumes continue to exert influence in judicial decision making. Under the present Constitution, both the Supreme Court and the Court of Appeal are each, a 'superior court of record'.[^note_art105] While all judgements of these courts are now digitally accessible, in general, when matters are dismissed, courts do not provide a written judgement.

**1.2.3	Barriers to Comprehensive Study**

Two significant barriers impede comprehensive assessment of Sri Lanka's apex courts. First, prior to 2012, only judgments selected by an editorial committee were published in the Law Reports; no comprehensive record exists of judgments from 1809 to 2011. Second, filing statistics are not publicly available, leaving researchers with access only to the number of judgments delivered, and only post-2011. These archival and transparency gaps are discussed further in section 4.

This raises important questions: (1) how does the law develop if the database of judgments is incomplete in a legal system that is reliant on judicial precedent? (2) is the jurisprudence that guides landmark decisions of the apex courts today an accurate representation of the cumulative jurisprudence that is unrecorded? and (3) can a discovery of these inadequacies through the use of computational methods change our perception of what we believe to be the content of the law? We aim to answer these questions to some extent through the application of computational methods to our data.

**1.3	Institutional Context 2012-2022**

When examining the work of the Sri Lankan appellate courts for this time period, it is necessary to bear in mind the relevant constitutional developments. The enactment of the 18th Amendment in 2010 had established the strongest Executive Presidency that Sri Lanka has had so far.[@edrisinha2011eighteenth] The 18th Amendment removed the two-year term limit of the President, repealed the Constitutional Council and replaced it with a Parliamentary Council. The President, who had full immunity during office, appointed justices of the appellate courts. From 2010 to 2015, the Government enjoyed a 2/3 majority in Parliament, the support required for amending the constitution as well as for enacting laws that are inconsistent with the constitution except the entrenched clauses.[^note_art83]

In 2012, the then Chief Justice was impeached despite resistance by the domestic legal community and international criticism.[@samararatne2022resilience, pp. 6 ff] During this year only 9 judgments were issued by the Supreme Court under Article 126.[@samararatne2017judicial, p. 39] Between 2011 and 2014, the total number of judgements issued under Article 126 was 50.[@samararatne2017judicial, p. 39] With the change of government in 2015, the 19th Amendment was enacted. Among other things, it recognized the right to information, provided that fundamental rights petitions may be filed to challenge the actions of the President and re-introduced a revised Constitutional Council.[@welikala2016nineteenth] The issue related to the purported impeachment of the Chief Justice was addressed through executive action.[@dailyft2015justice] Several of the appointments to the superior courts during this period received widespread acceptance. In contrast to the number of judgments on fundamental rights issued 2011 to 2014, the number issued between 2015 and 2020 was 227.[@samararatne2023judicial, p. 14]

The political cohabitation that was established between the President and the Prime Minister in 2015 eventually broke down during this time, as evidenced by the constitutional crisis of 2018.[@welikala2019constitutional] The President unconstitutionally removed the Prime Minister and appointed former President Mahinda Rajapaksa. Parliament was dissolved by the President prematurely. Two petitions were filed, in the Court of Appeal and Supreme Court respectively. The writ petition sought a writ of quo warranto against the new and purported Prime Minister while a fundamental rights petition was filed by the then Opposition claiming that the unconstitutional dissolution was a violation of the right to equality.[^note_2018_cases] In both cases, the claims were upheld and the status quo ante was restored by January 2019. However, in April 2019, the Easter Sunday attacks took place. Soon thereafter the Prime Minister alleged that he had not been invited to attend the meetings of the National Security Council.[@samararatne2022resilience, p. 12] Due to these developments, the 19th Amendment was perceived to be unworkable and unsuitable for Sri Lanka. Presidential candidate Gotabaya Rajapaksa pledged to repeal it and restore the Executive Presidency as it was established under the 1978 Constitution. Soon after winning the elections that year, the 19th Amendment was repealed. The 20th Amendment repealed the Constitutional Council and established (yet again) a Parliamentary Council.[@samararatne2023guarantor, pp. 265 ff]

During this same time, the Covid-19 pandemic spread, becoming a catalyst for another crisis in Sri Lanka which reached its peak in 2022 when the country defaulted on its foreign debt payments.[@moramudali2025foreign, p. 147] 2022 was a year of significant suffering across Sri Lanka due to shortages in electricity and LPG cooking gas among others. It was also a time of political and public unrest. These developments led to the unprecedented mobilization of the public who made many political demands, broadly described as demands for a 'system change'.[@uyangoda2024localising] The then President, Prime Minister and the Cabinet were compelled to resign. A new President was elected from Parliament as required by the Constitution.

2. **Use of Computational Methods for Study of Courts**

Computational methods have already been used to study courts of several jurisdictions.[@chandra2023court] Computational Social Science enables the collection and analysis of data at a scale previously unattainable.[@lazer2009computational, pp. 721-722] These methods are applied within a 'law as data' approach.[@frankenreiter2020computational, pp. 19, 42] They promise the same benefits that general quantitative legal research prioritises, but on a scale that is unparalleled and previously unattainable manually. The potential that computational methods hold for data analysis pertaining to a judicial system, however, must be evaluated with caution. These methods carry the same methodological constraints as quantitative empirical research generally: correlation cannot be assumed to indicate causation.[@khosla2022science] The sheer volume of data processable via computational methods runs the risk of concealing these limitations. The methodological constraints are discussed in section 4.

With due recognition of the constraints that apply, the potential that computational methods hold for the study of courts is substantial. While failing to demonstrate causal relations, the ability to process data in quantities far beyond what would be possible manually produces results that can both reveal the realities of the judicial system and discern patterns in judicial workload and output on a scale that would have previously been impossible. In doing so, the findings of research by way of computational methods could serve to confirm concerns or characteristics already suspected of the court system or provide direction towards areas warranting further investigation. Collectively, it holds promise to pave the path towards judicial and legal system reform where required. The necessity of interpreting the empirical alongside the normative and doctrinal, and not in isolation, only increases in importance during the formulation of any such reform.
