# Abstract

**First Steps: Empirical Study of Sri Lanka’s Apex Courts with a Focus on Public Law Litigation**

Elijah Hoole, Dinesha Samararatne, Yanitra Kumaraguru and Amanda Halliday

*What is the impact of the day to day issuance of judgements by a court on  the development of the law and on the legal system more broadly? How should that impact be identified and examined? This article is an early attempt to use computational methods to answer these questions. We have compiled a database to interpret the jurisprudence of Sri Lanka’s appellate courts between 2012 – 2022\. The corpus, CDTextLK, contains 1278 Supreme Court decisions and 7314 Court of Appeal decisions. By applying computational methods to CDTextLK we identify eight patterns or insights that enable us to present a more granular, realistic account of the work of Sri Lanka’s appellate courts. They include descriptive statistics and visualisations on the performance of the courts such as average time taken for a decision, average length of decision, and proportion of allowed and dismissed cases as well as mutual information analysis and topic modelling to reveal some latent themes in decision texts. These insights help us to reveal and demonstrate the ordinary life and effect of the appellate courts, challenge received wisdom on how to study courts, where to look for knowledge and ultimately, disrupts our traditional assessment of  court decision. These findings and implications, we argue, provide a necessary corrective to the knowledge that has hitherto been generated through reliance on judicial precedent and landmark cases in Sri Lanka.* 

Key take aways:

1. The Court of Appeal delivers approximately four times more judgments than the Supreme Court annually, yet its jurisprudence remains systematically understudied – raising questions about where doctrinal development actually occurs and whether scholarly focus on Supreme Court precedent alone provides an accurate account of appellate judicial impact.

2. Fundamental rights petitions take an average of 4.17 years to resolve, despite a constitutional mandate of two months – revealing a substantial gap between constitutional aspiration and institutional reality that undermines the effectiveness of this remedy.

3. Land disputes and state employment matters dominate appellate court workload, not the constitutional questions typically featured in legal scholarship – suggesting that discourse on judicial reform and case management may be addressing the wrong problems.

4. Writ jurisdiction significantly exceeds fundamental rights jurisdiction numerically (1,741 CA writ decisions vs. 361 SC FR decisions during 2012-2022), yet receives minimal scholarly attention despite the low ratio of Supreme Court appeals to Court of Appeal writ decisions (31:1,741) indicating that the Court of Appeal serves as the effective final forum in the overwhelming majority of writ cases – an empirical reality that challenges assumptions about where judicial impact occurs and which remedies provide routine access to justice.

5. Once leave to proceed is granted in fundamental rights cases, 62% result in judgments favouring petitioners – indicating that the leave stage functions as the primary gatekeeping mechanism and warrants closer examination.

6. Variations in Supreme Court output between 2012–2015 and 2016–2022 correlate with constitutional developments affecting judicial independence – demonstrating that empirical patterns can expose relationships between institutional design and judicial performance.

Keywords: empirical legal research; computational methods; judicial statistics; Sri Lanka; Supreme Court; Court of Appeal; fundamental rights; writ jurisdiction; topic modelling; legal empiricism; appellate courts; access to justice; case law analysis; judicial performance
