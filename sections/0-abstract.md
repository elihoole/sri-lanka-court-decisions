# Abstract {.unnumbered}

**First Steps: Empirical Study of Sri Lanka's Apex Courts with a Focus on Public Law Litigation**

Elijah Hoole, Dinesha Samararatne, Yanitra Kumaraguru and Amanda Halliday

*What is the impact of the day to day issuance of judgements by a court on  the development of the law and on the legal system more broadly? How should that impact be identified and examined? This article is an early attempt to use computational methods to answer these questions. We have compiled a database to interpret the jurisprudence of Sri Lanka's appellate courts between 2012 – 2022.* \texttt{SriLankaCourtDecisions} *contains 1278 Supreme Court decisions and 7314 Court of Appeal decisions. By applying computational methods to* \texttt{SriLankaCourtDecisions} *we identify six key insights that enable us to present a more granular, realistic account of the work of Sri Lanka's appellate courts. They include descriptive statistics and visualisations on the performance of the courts such as average time taken for a decision, average length of decision, and proportion of allowed and dismissed cases as well as mutual information analysis and topic modelling to reveal some latent themes in decision texts. These insights help us to reveal and demonstrate the ordinary life and effect of the appellate courts, challenge received wisdom on how to study courts, where to look for knowledge and ultimately, disrupts our traditional assessment of  court decision. These findings and implications, we argue, provide a necessary corrective to the knowledge that has hitherto been generated through reliance on judicial precedent and landmark cases in Sri Lanka.* 

Key take aways:

1. The Court of Appeal delivers nearly six times more judgments than the Supreme Court annually, yet its jurisprudence remains systematically understudied – raising questions about where doctrinal development actually occurs and whether scholarly focus on Supreme Court precedent alone provides an accurate account of appellate judicial impact.

2. Writ petitions vastly outnumber fundamental rights petitions (1,741 CA writ decisions vs. 361 SC fundamental rights rulings during 2012-2022), yet writ jurisprudence remains comparatively underdeveloped. The Court of Appeal serves as the effective final forum in the overwhelming majority of writ cases. During the study period, the Supreme Court decided only 31 appeals from Court of Appeal writ judgments whereas the Court of Appeal pronounced verdicts on 1,741 writ application. 

3. Fundamental rights petitions take an average of 4.17 years to resolve, despite a constitutional mandate of two months. Court of Appeal writs resolve faster (mean 3.15 years) despite having no constitutional time mandate – an inversion suggesting that formal rules matter less than institutional capacity and procedural culture.

4. Topic modelling identifies land disputes and state employment matters as the dominant themes in appellate jurisprudence, not the constitutional questions typically featured in legal scholarship – suggesting that discourse on judicial reform and case management may be addressing the wrong problems.

5. Variations in Supreme Court output between 2012–2015 and 2016–2022 correlate with constitutional developments affecting judicial independence, particularly the 19th Amendment – demonstrating that empirical patterns can expose relationships between institutional and political contexts and judicial performance.

6. Linguistic patterns and decision length empirically confirm doctrinal hierarchies: Supreme Court decisions employ the language of error and formal citation practices that reflect apex court authority, while fundamental rights judgments are substantially longer than writ decisions, revealing the differential judicial labour allocated to constitutional versus administrative disputes.

Keywords: empirical legal research; computational methods; judicial statistics; Sri Lanka; Supreme Court; Court of Appeal; fundamental rights; writ jurisdiction; topic modelling; legal empiricism; appellate courts; access to justice; case law analysis; judicial performance
