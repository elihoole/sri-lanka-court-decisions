# Abstract {.unnumbered}

**First Steps: Empirical Study of Public Law Litigation in Sri Lanka's Apex Courts**

Elijah Hoole, Dinesha Samararatne, Yanitra Kumaraguru

**Key Insights:**

1. The Court of Appeal delivers nearly six times more judgments than the Supreme Court annually, yet, its jurisprudence remains systematically understudied. This gap suggests a gap in knowledge on actual judicial impact. It raises questions about where doctrinal development actually occurs and whether scholarly focus on Supreme Court precedent alone provides an accurate account of appellate judicial impact.

2. In these two courts, writ petitions vastly outnumber fundamental rights petitions (1,741 CA writ decisions vs. 361 SC fundamental rights rulings during 2012-2022), yet writ jurisprudence remains comparatively underdeveloped. Furthermore, during the study period, the Supreme Court decided only 31 appeals from Court of Appeal writ judgments whereas the Court of Appeal pronounced verdicts on 1,741 writ application. This data suggests that the Court of Appeal serves as the effective final forum in the overwhelming majority of writ cases.

3. The Supreme Court takes an average of 4.17 years to resolve Fundamental Rights petitions, despite a constitutional rule that requires such petitions to be concluded in two months. Court of Appeal resolves writs faster (mean 3.15 years) despite having no such constitutional time mandate. This inversion suggests that for time taken for the conclusion of these cases, formal rules matter less than institutional capacity, procedural culture or professional practices.

4. Topic modelling identifies land disputes and state employment matters as the dominant themes in appellate jurisprudence, not the constitutional questions typically featured in legal scholarship. This insight offers helpful guidance to efforts on institutional reform including case management.

5. The numerical variations in Supreme Court judgements between 2012–2015 and 2016–2022 correlate with constitutional developments affecting judicial independence, particularly the 19th Amendment. This insight demonstrates that empirical patterns can expose relationships between institutional and political contexts and judicial performance.

6. The linguistic patterns and decision length empirically identified in these judgements confirm doctrinal hierarchies: Supreme Court decisions employ the language of error and formal citation practices that reflect apex court authority, while fundamental rights judgments are substantially longer than writ decisions, revealing the differential judicial labour allocated to constitutional versus administrative disputes.

**Keywords:** empirical legal research; computational methods; judicial statistics; Sri Lanka; Supreme Court; Court of Appeal; fundamental rights; writ jurisdiction; topic modelling; legal empiricism; appellate courts; access to justice; case law analysis; judicial performance
