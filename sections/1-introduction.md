# **Introduction**

In common-law jurisdictions, judicial precedents – formally understood as appellate rulings that bind lower courts on questions of law – determine the content of the law. In practice, however, precedent is often conflated with a narrower set of influential 'landmark' judgments that come to dominate how the law is perceived and interpreted. Sri Lanka, as a common-law jurisdiction, reflects this pattern. Computational methods – the use of data science and artificial intelligence to identify patterns across large datasets – offer a way to move beyond this narrow focus by enabling systematic analysis of the full corpus of case law. These methods enable analysis of large corpora that would be impractical to undertake manually. In doing so, they generate new forms of knowledge that extend beyond jurisprudence driven by landmark rulings. Such knowledge has the potential to unsettle received assumptions and ingrained perceptions about the workings of courts. Put differently, computational analysis can make visible dimensions of judicial activity that landmark-focused approaches often obscure.

This article is a first attempt to bring such empirical analysis into the doctrinal and normative study of Sri Lanka's appellate courts. We apply computational methods to analyse judgments of the Supreme Court and Court of Appeal from 2012 to 2022, drawing on a corpus derived from judgments made available online by each court. Using exploratory data analysis, mutual information analysis, and topic modelling, we address several questions: Does the landmark judgment paradigm accurately represent the substance and volume of appellate work? How effectively do Sri Lanka's apex courts function as institutions for access to justice? What can patterns in judicial output reveal about the relative institutional significance of each court, and about the effects of constitutional change on judicial independence?

[Section 2](#sec:empirical-study) situates this study within the tradition of legal empiricism and judicial statistics research, describes Sri Lanka's mixed legal system and the structure of its Superior Courts, and examines the judicial branch from 2012 to 2022 in the context of constitutional developments. [Section 3](#sec:dataset) describes the compilation of the `SriLankaCourtDecisions` corpus and the technical challenges encountered. [Section 4](#sec:insights) presents empirical patterns identified through our computational analysis, covering the quantitative dominance of the Court of Appeal, the prevalence of writ petitions, systematic delays in delivering judgments, the dominance of land and employment disputes, and variations in judicial output correlating with constitutional developments. [Section 5](#sec:limitations) addresses the methodological, temporal, archival, and technical limitations that constrain this study and discusses their implications for the claims advanced. [Section 6](#sec:conclusion) concludes by summarising the contribution of `SriLankaCourtDecisions` and acknowledging the preliminary nature of these findings, while [Section 7](#sec:future-work) identifies directions for future empirical research.
