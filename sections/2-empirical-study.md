2. # **Empirical Study of Sri Lankan Courts**

Legal empiricism has been defined as research that includes 'systematic, observational study of law, whether quantitative or qualitative, contemporary or historical, direct or archival'.[^1] Within this broad area of legal research, the study of ‘judicial behavior’ has been a focus from its early days.[^2] Data on court cases has been described as ‘judicial statistics’ and early efforts at reporting on such statistics is dated back to 1872, in the United States.[^3] An early example of research on judicial statistics is *The Business of the Supreme Court: A Study in the Federal Judicial System*.[^4] This type of research has also been described as the study of judicial behaviour and broadly covers judicial personalities, judicial attitudes and case load.[^5] Realists took the view that 'judgments were influenced by personal and social factors, and ought therefore to be analysed not only with reference to statute, precedent, and established legal principle, but also with reference to judges' social backgrounds and political beliefs.'[^6] One of the aims of an empirical approach here is to test received wisdom on theories about the work of the appellate courts in Sri Lanka.[^7]

The study of jurisprudence of the Sri Lankan apex courts has rarely been supplemented with quantitative analysis. Kishali Pinto Jayawardene's work on the writ of habeas corpus and the work of one of our authors with regard to fundamental rights judgments are two early examples.[^8] In her work, Jayawardene offers a quantitative and qualitative analysis of 644 habeas corpus writ petitions. Jayawardene's work established that the habeas corpus writ is ineffective in Sri Lanka, despite the influential jurisprudence in this jurisdiction such as the *Leeda Violet* case and the *Plantation Point* camp case.[^9] In *Leeda Violet*, the Court of Appeal held that where a person has been taken into custody and can no longer be found, the state will be held responsible and ordered that compensation be paid. The Court relied on the well-known case of *Velasquez Rodriguez v Honduras* in developing its reasoning. A similar approach was followed in the *Plantation Point* case. These two cases represent a significant development of jurisprudence. However, Jayawardene’s empirical study demonstrates that for more than 90% of petitioners (mostly family members of disappeared persons), the writ of habeas corpus has been ineffective. This finding raises significant questions about how we define jurisprudence and judicial precedent. We return to these questions later in this article. 

Similarly, through a simple descriptive analysis of the type of fundamental right most commonly invoked in judgments on fundamental rights since 2006, one of our authors has established two key findings. One is that the most commonly invoked fundamental right is Art 12, that is the right to equality. The second is that this Article is overwhelmingly invoked by public servants to challenge recruitments, transfers or promotions.[^10] Here too, there is an interesting contrast between jurisprudence and judicial precedent as interpreted through the conventional approach to law and the empirical examination of cases. Jurisprudentially, significant developments have been achieved by the Sri Lankan judiciary with regard to several fundamental rights. For instance, courts have recognized that the right to be free from torture includes the right to be free from sexual violence. Courts have recognized new rights, such as the rights of persons with disabilities and rights to the environment. However, the empirical study of Sri Lanka’s fundamental rights case-law reveals that this remedy is sought overwhelmingly by public servants and much of the Court’s time and resources are spent on reviewing decisions of the public service regarding recruitment, transfers and promotions. A more recent example is the work by Sandani Abeyawardene on topic modeling of judgments of two supreme courts: that of Sri Lanka (2013-2020) and the United Kingdom (2009 – 2022).[^11] Abeyawardene uses Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) with Machine Learning for Language Toolkit (MALLET) to identify the most significant topics in the jurisprudence of each jurisdiction. 

Beyond these contributions, empirical legal research in Sri Lanka has been rare.[^12] Neelan Tiruchelvam’s work on mediation boards is perhaps an early example of empirical legal work.[^13] Tiruchelvam’s work is also identified as an example of the induction of the law and society approach to the law in Sri Lanka. More recent examples of this approach would be one of our author’s work on pioneering women in Sri Lanka’s legal profession and on Sri Lanka’s first Election Commission.[^14] 

The limitations to this approach to research are well established. Unlike in natural sciences, even with data, we cannot establish causal inference and we cannot study the counterfactual.[^15] What we can offer, in this instance, are descriptive statistical inferences and making our dataset publicly available for verification and/or further study.[^16] A point of comparison may be the US Supreme Court Database.[^17] This database is supported by the American National Science Foundation. It contains the cases decided by the US Supreme Court 1791 – 2022 and offers over 'two hundred pieces of information about each case' including the votes of the justices.[^18] Another point of reference is the Concourtstats of South Africa, a project which provides empirical analysis of the jurisprudence and work of the Constitutional Court of South Africa.[^19]

The quantitative perspective we offer here of the jurisprudence of the Sri Lankan Courts 2012-2022 can be interpreted only when combined with doctrinal analysis. The limited empirical analysis we offer here does not probe the way in which legal or constitutional doctrine is developed or affected, nor does it query the normative approaches adopted in jurisprudence. The analysis offered here is exploratory and descriptive. The triangulation of quantitative analysis with qualitative analysis remains for future research. There are further prospects for this type of research, which we note here. The empirical assessment may be analysed in relation to qualitative influences on judicial decision making (known in language studies as content analysis) – whether it be specific legal doctrines, judicial dispensations, role of litigators or types of petitioners. 

## **The Legal System**

Sri Lanka’s modern legal system has been shaped by its encounters with colonialism. Today it is one of the three mixed legal systems in the world.[^26] Between 1505 CE to 1948 CE, a long period of 443 years, Sri Lanka was under partial colonial rule of the Portuguese, the Dutch, and, finally, under the complete control of the British. In 1972, it became a republic. During this time, the colonisers established court systems, introduced their laws and established legal institutions.[^27] The Portuguese had the least amount of territorial as well as political control whereas the Dutch had significant control over Sri Lanka’s maritime provinces.[^28] The British, unlike the other two colonial powers, gained and maintained total control over the whole island for over 130 years. Sri Lanka was a crown colony since 1802. Sri Lanka’s Supreme Court, established in 1801 is known to be one the oldest in the region and Sri Lanka was the first crown colony to have a jury system. 

Due to the establishment of a court system by the Dutch, the Roman-Dutch law was established as Sri Lanka’s common law. Today, the Roman-Dutch law  applies in relation to property and persons while English common law is recognized as the law that may be used to fill in lacunae in legislation. During its long colonial rule, the British laid a firm foundation for Sri Lanka’s legal system. The Dutch recognized, codified and applied what was described as ‘personal laws’ or laws/customs of distinct ethnic/religious communities in the Island; the Tesawalamai, Muslim law and the Kandyan law.[^29] In keeping with their general policies, the British continued to recognize these laws and the Roman-Dutch law that had been introduced by the Dutch. Selectively and systematically, the British introduced legislation and English common law during their rule. This resulted in a complex and mixed legal system that continues to remain in place. Sri Lanka declared itself a Republic in 1972 and has since then had two constitutions. The current Constitution, enacted in 1978 has been amended 21 times. 

## **Development of Law**

In this context, the law in Sri Lanka is developed through multiple avenues: constitutional reform, legislation, case law, regulations and sometimes even through policies. Case law is the primary means of developing the law in several areas including constitutional law, administrative law, criminal law, land law, family law and the law of damages. In the case of public law, judicial precedent determines how the relevant legal basis (constitution, legislation or the common law) is applied. In the case of land law and family law, legislation applies along with the Roman Dutch law. The law of damages is governed primarily by the Roman Dutch law. Case law is central to the development of the law both in the substantive and procedural sense in Sri Lanka.

However, the way in which these laws are developed by the courts differs. In the case *of Priyani Soysa v Arsecularatne*, the Supreme Court observed that even following British occupation of Dutch territories in Sri Lanka, Roman-Dutch Law continued to be in force, only subject to structural alteration by the legislature, and not the judiciary, due to the Proclamation of 23 September 1799. English Common Law on the other hand, has a marked presence in several areas of law, including Criminal, Contract, Commercial, Constitutional and Administrative Law. As a result of the widespread influence of English Common Law in Sri Lanka, several English Law principles, notably that of judicial precedent, characterises Sri Lankan jurisprudence. Customary Laws in Sri Lanka have limited application in the spheres of marriage, divorce and succession, and are applicable only to Sri Lankans whose ethnicity corresponds with one of the several customary laws (Tesawalamai, Kandyan or Muslim Law).

As a result of this complex composition, case law is significant in Sri Lanka. Sri Lanka's highest appellate courts, the Supreme Court and the Court of Appeal, are the final arbiters on questions relating to the application and interpretation of Roman-Dutch Law, English Common Law, the Customary Laws as well as the interpretation of the Constitution. To assess how Sri Lanka's 'apex courts' resolve questions relating to three different legal frameworks that comprise Sri Lanka's legal system, analysis of judgments is required. Bearing this aspect in mind is essential when employing empirical methods, including computational methods, for the study of Sri Lanka's jurisprudence.

## **Superior Courts**

Sri Lanka’s superior courts are the Supreme Court and the Court of Appeal.[^34] These courts are located in the superior courts complex in Hulftsdorp, in the capital of the country.[^35] The Judicature Act identifies the courts of record in Sri Lanka.[^36] However, only select judgments of the Supreme Court are published officially in Sri Lanka’s case reports, the Sri Lanka Law Reports (Sri L R) starting from the year 1978. 

Judgments of the Supreme Court and the Court of Appeal from the years 1809 to 1978 are recorded in volumes titled the 'Ceylon New Law Reports'. Although Sri Lanka gained independence from Britain in 1948, it remained under the Dominion of the British Crown and was known as the Dominion of Ceylon. It was only in 1972 upon the adoption of an autochthonous Republican Constitution, that Sri Lanka gained complete independence from the British Crown and became the Democratic Socialist Republic of Sri Lanka. Starting from 1978, upon the adoption of the Second Republican Constitution, select judgments of the Supreme Court were published in volumes titled the 'Sri Lanka Law Reports'. The judgments that would be reported in this way were selected by a committee of Justices of the Court, headed by the Chief Justice. Physical copies of judgments recorded in both the Ceylon New Law Reports and the Sri Lanka Law Reports are available at the National Library in Colombo, and digital copies are available on the website run by the Ministry of Justice ([https://www.lawnet.gov.lk](https://www.lawnet.gov.lk)). Since 2012, all judgments of both apex courts are also available as digital copies, accessible on the official government websites of the Supreme Court ([https://www.supremecourt.lk](https://www.supremecourt.lk)) and the Court of Appeal ([https://www.courtofappeal.lk](https://www.courtofappeal.lk)).[^37] This was a significant milestone in the improvement of access to the jurisprudence of the apex courts. Prior to this, accessing judgments presented practical challenges. Until a case was reported, access to the judgment could only be through a party to the case or through a formal application to the relevant court registry along with the payment of the required fees. Unreported judgments were not accessible except through personal knowledge of such case or through media reports. 

One of the barriers to a comprehensive assessment of the judicial approaches of Sri Lanka's apex courts is the incomplete reporting of judgments prior to 2012. From 1809 to 2011, the Law Reports feature judgments selected by an editorial committee under the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court and the President of the Court of Appeal. As a consequence of this, there is no source or database in Sri Lanka which captures the entire gamut of judgments issued by the apex courts from 1809 to 2011. What is available to the public, from this 200 year period prior to digitisation, are only those judgments which the editorial committee, based on opaque processes, determined worthy of inclusion in the Law Reports.

The official government websites for both the Supreme Court and the Court of Appeal have published judgments made by the apex courts from 2009 onwards. Sri Lanka Law Reports continue to be published and the judgments reported in those volumes continue to exert influence in judicial decision making. Under the present Constitution, both the Supreme Court and the Court of Appeal are each, a ‘superior court of record’. Accordingly, they are courts whose acts and judicial proceedings are to be ‘permanently maintained and recorded’. It is questionable whether Sri Lanka’s apex courts have functioned as courts of record from 1809 to 2011. Furthermore, even post-2011, judgments on leave to proceed or appeal are not comprehensively recorded digitally.

Another barrier to a comprehensive study is the lack of information on how many cases or petitions are filed before the apex courts. Presumably, the Court Registrars are aware of this number; however, it is not released to the public. Consequently, the public only has access to the number of judgments a court delivers, and only post-2011.

These two barriers to the detailed study of the work of the Supreme Court and Court of Appeal highlight the lack of transparency in court processes. This is particularly problematic given the courts in question are the apex courts in a mixed legal system.

This raises important questions: (1) how does the law develop if the database of judgments is incomplete in a legal system that is reliant on judicial precedent, (2) is the jurisprudence that guides landmark decisions of the apex courts today an accurate representation of the cumulative jurisprudence that is unrecorded? and (3) can a discovery of these inadequacies through the use of computational methods change our perception of what we believe to be the content of the law?

**Judicial Branch 2012 to 2022**

When examining the work of the Sri Lankan appellate courts for this time period, it is necessary to bear in mind the constitutional developments of the time period. The enactment of the 18th Amendment in 2010 had established the strongest Executive Presidency that Sri Lanka has had so far. The 18th Amendment removed the two-year term limit of the President, repealed the Constitutional Council and replaced it with a Parliamentary Council. The President, who had full immunity during office, appointed justices of the appellate courts. From 2010 to 2015, the Government enjoyed a 2/3 majority in Parliament, the support required for amending the constitution as well as for enacting laws that are inconsistent with the constitution except the entrenched clauses.

## **Use of Computational Methods for Study of Courts**

Where empirical legal research is rare and data-driven studies of apex court jurisprudence scarce in Sri Lanka, the use of computational methods for the study of courts in the country remains untapped; with the exception of the work done in topic modelling the judgments of the Supreme Court by Sandani Abeyawardene and the recent efforts by the authors of this paper. Due to the limitations in the digitalisation of judgments and court documents in Sri Lanka, any immediate use of computational methods for the study of courts is inherently confined to the judgments of the apex courts of the country that were issued during a limited period of time.

In contrast, computational methods have already been used to study courts of several jurisdictions.[^20] Some researchers have drawn from the data processed to formulate recommendations for court reform.[^21] Yet, in several ways, researchers and court watchers are at the early stages of exploring the utility of computational methods.

Computational Social Science enables the collection and analysis of data at a scale previously unattainable.[^22]

Application of computational methods within a ‘law as data’ approach.[^23]

These methods promise the same benefits that general quantitative legal research prioritises, but on a scale that is unparalleled and previously unattainable manually. The potential that computational methods hold for data analysis pertaining to a judicial system, however, must be evaluated with caution. These methods also carry the same limitations that must be associated with quantitative empirical research: statistical pictures illustrate correlation but cannot be assumed to indicate causation, and therefore are inadvisable to be considered in isolation.[^24] The sheer amount of data that can be captured and processed via computational methods, however, runs the risk of concealing these limitations, resulting in a greater likelihood of researchers neglecting to recognize or address them. The employment of advanced forms of machine learning in processing data may pose a similar risk.

With due recognition of the limitations described above, the potential that computational methods hold for the study of courts is substantial. While failing to demonstrate causal relations, the ability to process data in quantities far beyond what would be possible manually produces results that can both reveal the realities of the judicial system and discern patterns in judicial workload and output on a scale that would have previously been impossible. In doing so, the findings of research by way of computational methods could serve to confirm concerns or characteristics already suspected of the court system[^25] or provide direction towards areas warranting further investigation. Collectively, it holds promise to pave the path towards judicial and legal system reform where required. The necessity of interpreting the empirical alongside the normative and doctrinal, and not in isolation, only increases in importance during the formulation of any such reform.

## **Legal system digitisation: limitations and challenges**

Computational methods may be applied where digitised legal texts exist and are accessible for research. In Sri Lanka, the bulk of legal documents – judgments, legal textbooks, copies of petitions, acts of law – are not available digitally. As described above, the higher appellate courts began publishing their judgments as PDF files starting from 2012. Petitions are still filed physically, notices are issued via post, and only recently have the courts started entertaining digital evidence. As such only a fraction of Sri Lanka’s legal material is available in computable form. It follows that what we can learn about the country’s legal system by applying computational methods is therefore quite limited.

What is available digitally is not complete nor error-free. There is duplication on both appellate court websites: a judgment made in 2017 may be listed again under 2018. Case numbers are not standardised, and while some aspects of (re)standardisation are easy: for instance, correcting the year substring '11' to its standard form '2011' is straightforward. Others, such as errors in the application type part of the case number, can only be manually fixed. Some judgments are image-only or scanned pdfs and need to be read via OCR systems which invariably introduce further corrupting artefacts. These are just a few of the preliminary and preventable challenges that complicate an exercise as simple as counting the number of fundamental rights applications in the Supreme Court for a given year via a computer programme. There are other deeper and more technical challenges. For instance, even though the apex courts function in English, judgments often quote substantial passages of vernacular text from lower court decisions. Sri Lankan names confound best-in-class named entity recognition models. Critical metadata is buried within unstructured judgment texts.

A further limitation that must be borne in mind when assessing the findings discussed here is that the empirical data is the data available on the official websites of the Supreme Court and Court of Appeal. The paper relies on the official websites for the coverage of these judgments. However, courts issue many different orders, including interlocutory orders, interim relief, enjoining orders, and final judgments. To get a full picture, one would need to look at all of these, but our empirical study only looks at judgments. Furthermore, there is an inconsistency in how the courts use "orders" versus "judgments". The Court of Appeal website currently has separate sections for judgments and orders, and often final judgments are published under the "Orders" section. This means that a portion of the court's output might be missed if only the "judgments" section is scraped.

Although we have attempted to address several of these challenges in our present work, the findings therefore may remain affected by their residuary impact and other unknown issues.
