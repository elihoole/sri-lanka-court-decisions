To approximate the date on which an application was filed, we extracted the year in which the application related to a decision was filed from the application (or case) number. Assuming that filing of applications is distributed uniformly through the year, we approximated the date of filing as {extracted\_year}\-June-30 for all cases across the two courts. Making the same assumption, we approximated the date of decision delivery in the Court of Appeal as {given\_year}\-{given\_month}\-15. We then calculated the time taken to deliver a decision as the difference between the date of decision delivery and the date of application filing. Dismissed vs. allowed

*Proportion of decisions dismissed vs. allowed.*

Different rules apply to the different types of cases that come before the Supreme Court or Court of Appeal in terms of whether they may be dismissed or allowed. Dismissal, in this section, means dismissal after a full hearing. These cases include all judgements issued by these courts, not only writs and FR. 

In the case of writs and FR petitions, proceedings are in two stages. When a petition has been filed, the Court initially conducts a hearing to determine whether leave to proceed will be granted. At this stage, it is possible for a petition to be dismissed. However, whether or not a judgement or order is issued to record a dismissal, is at the discretion of the Courts. Where leave to proceed is granted, Court may also grant interim relief sought by the petitioner. In such instances, Court issues an order. For our present purposes, we note that the CDTextLK does not include data on dismissals at the leave to proceed stage as that data is not produced as a written record. To that extent the data on dismissal have a significant limitation as it does not depict the accurate number of dismissals by courts. 

Whether a petition may be upheld or dismissed depends on a range of factors. These include, the strength of the petitioner’s claim, the strength of the applicable legal or constitutional doctrine, the quality of submissions made to court (both written and oral submissions) and the exercise of judicial discretion. Therefore, whether a petition may be upheld or dismissed cannot be predicted based on any empirical pattern. \[WHAT THEN, IS THE SIGNIFICANCE OF THIS DATA?\] – does not reflect on jurisprudence or work of the court. However, perceptions abound about judicial attitudes overall to petitions writs/FR – that courts are prone one way or another. Empirical methods can be used to verify these perceptions.

Token-level mutual information analysis

Following the steps outlined in , we performed mutual information analysis on the Supreme Court and Court of Appeal decisions to identify the most salient words in each corpus. Mutual information analysis is where we quantify the presence or absence of words within a corpus. The frequency of the words used has the potential to reveal attributes of the jurisprudence of both courts. For example… These empirical patterns can provide helpful entry points to analyse the jurisprudence of these courts too. We followed the same tokenisation scheme (breaking down the specific words) as in Section [4.3](#bookmark=id.z7ou7uqtzwkn) but further stemmed each token. We used a simple add 1 smoothing scheme to avoid zero probabilities. Top-15 tokens with the highest mutual information scores are tabulated in Table [\[tab:token-stats\]](#bookmark=id.s6zo1v3r4n33).

The most salient word was ‘grant’ which appeared in 91% of Supreme Court decisions but only in 32% of Court of Appeal decisions. The underlying reason for the high information content of this word is somewhat trivial though: Supreme Court decisions routinely reference the granting of leave to appeal – both the grounds on which leave was granted as well as the date on which it was granted – in the decision text.

The words ‘answer’, ‘question’, and ‘law’ having a high prevalence in Supreme Court decisions owes to the fact that the court is often called upon to answer questions of law. Although the Court of Appeal is also an appellate court, the same words have a lower prevalence in its decisions.

The word ‘err’ appears in 24% of Supreme Court decisions but only in 1% of Court of Appeal decisions. On closer inspection, we observed that the word appears in the context of Supreme Court justices holding a lower court judge to have erred in their judgement. Such language is almost never used in Court of Appeal decisions despite the court’s appellate jurisdiction.

The high prevalence of the words ‘paragraph’ and ‘refer’ in Supreme Court decisions arises in the context of the court referring to specific sections of petitions, the constitution, and other legislation and hints at a more formal, perhaps rigorous, style of writing in the Supreme Court compared to the Court of Appeal.

We applied Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) to CDTextLK in order to understand the latent topics and their word distributions present in court decisions. We used the Gensim  implementation of LDA with the following parameters: 20 topics and random seed of 0 with all other values set to defaults. In addition to tokenizing and stemming, we removed all tokens that appeared in less than 20 documents as well as tokens that recurred in more than 50% of documents. We also removed all tokens that were less than 3 characters long.

To find the three most common topics, across Supreme Court and Court of Appeal decisions, we, first, ran LDA on the entirety of CDTextLK under the experiment conditions described above. Then, we computed the average probability score for each of the 20 topics identified by LDA under the two sub corpora, independently, by summing the document-topic probability for a given topic for each document in the sub corpus and dividing by the total number of documents in the sub corpus. We obtained the three most common topics by summing the average probability scores for each topic across the two sub corpora and selecting the three topics with the highest scores. The top-40 tokens associated with each of the three topics are tabulated in Table [3](#bookmark=id.685o3n45iaak). We observe that Topic 1 concerns land and property disputes, Topic 2 captures state employment and promotion dispute related writs, and Topic 3 covers criminal proceedings.
