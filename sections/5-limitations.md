# **Limitations** {#sec:limitations}

This study confronts limitations inherent to empirical legal research generally and to the specific constraints of legal system digitization in Sri Lanka. These limitations operate at multiple levels: methodological, temporal, archival, technical, and analytical. We address each in turn, noting how they constrain the claims advanced in this article and the research questions they leave open for future investigation.

## Methodological Limitations

The limitations of empirical legal research are well established. Unlike in natural sciences, even with data, we cannot establish causal inference and we cannot study the counterfactual.[@epstein2014introduction, pp. 6-7] What we can offer, in this instance, are descriptive statistical inferences and making our dataset publicly available for verification and further study.[@epstein2014introduction, pp. 11-12, 15] Statistical pictures illustrate correlation but cannot be assumed to indicate causation, and therefore are inadvisable to be considered in isolation. The sheer amount of data that can be captured and processed via computational methods, however, runs the risk of concealing these limitations, resulting in a greater likelihood of researchers neglecting to recognize or address them.

The quantitative perspective we offer here of the jurisprudence of the Sri Lankan Courts 2012-2022 can be interpreted only when combined with doctrinal analysis. The limited empirical analysis we offer here does not probe the way in which legal or constitutional doctrine is developed or affected, nor does it query the normative approaches adopted in jurisprudence. The analysis offered here is exploratory and descriptive. The triangulation of quantitative analysis with qualitative analysis remains for future research. There are further prospects for this type of research, which we note here. The empirical assessment may be analysed in relation to qualitative influences on judicial decision making (known in language studies as content analysis) - whether it be specific legal doctrines, judicial dispensations, role of litigators or types of petitioners.

## Temporal Scope

The dataset's temporal scope (2012-2022) represents only a fraction of Sri Lankan legal history and excludes significant earlier periods that shaped the country's jurisprudence. Although the Supreme Court website lists judgments from 2009 onwards, the years 2009-2011 contain incomplete data, likely due to retrospective uploading practices. The Court of Appeal entries begin in 2012. Data availability thus determined our choice of time period. Due to the limitations in the digitization of judgments and court documents in Sri Lanka, any immediate use of computational methods for the study of courts is inherently confined to the judgments of the apex courts of the country that were issued during a limited period of time.

## Data Availability and Archival Gaps

One of the barriers to a comprehensive assessment of the judicial approaches of Sri Lanka's apex courts is the incomplete reporting of judgments prior to 2012. From 1809 to 2011, the Law Reports feature judgments selected by an editorial committee under the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court and the President of the Court of Appeal. As a consequence of this, there is no source or database in Sri Lanka which captures the entire gamut of judgments issued by the apex courts from 1809 to 2011. What is available to the public, from this 200 year period prior to digitization, are only those judgments which the editorial committee, based on opaque processes, determined worthy of inclusion in the Law Reports.

Another barrier to a comprehensive study is the lack of information on how many cases or petitions are filed before the apex courts. Presumably, the Court Registrars are aware of this number; however, it is not released to the public. Consequently, the public only has access to the number of judgments a court delivers, and only post-2011. Without filing statistics, we cannot calculate dismissal rates, disposition rates, or the proportion of filed cases that result in published judgments. This gap significantly limits our ability to assess court performance comprehensively.

These two barriers to the detailed study of the work of the Supreme Court and Court of Appeal highlight the lack of transparency in court processes. This is particularly problematic given the courts in question are the apex courts in a mixed legal system.

## Data Quality and Digitization Challenges

What is available digitally is not complete nor error-free. Both websites contained substantial duplicate entries. Following deduplication based on case number and decision date, the corpus comprised 1278 Supreme Court judgments and 7314 Court of Appeal judgments. The deduplication process identified duplicates through exact matching of case numbers combined with decision dates; in instances where the same case number appeared with different dates, we retained the entry with the more complete judgment text. While we conducted deduplication and quality checks, we cannot guarantee that all duplicates were identified or that all extracted text perfectly represents the original PDF documents.

Case numbers are not standardized, and while some aspects of (re)standardization are easy - for instance, correcting the year substring '11' to its standard form '2011' is straightforward - others, such as errors in the application type part of the case number, can only be manually fixed. Some judgments are image-only or scanned pdfs and needed to be read via OCR systems which invariably introduce further corrupting artefacts. A small number of judgments (fewer than 10 across the entire collection) required OCR conversion; we excluded these to maintain consistency in text extraction methodology and data quality. This exclusion, while maintaining data quality, may introduce slight bias if these judgments differ systematically from those successfully converted.

There are other deeper and more technical challenges. For instance, even though the apex courts function in English, judgments often quote substantial passages of vernacular text from lower court decisions. Sri Lankan names confound best-in-class named entity recognition models. Critical metadata is buried within unstructured judgment texts.

Extracting structured data from the court websites presented several challenges. Judge and bench information, while generally present, appears inconsistently across judgments - sometimes in formal headers, sometimes within the judgment text itself. We extracted this information using a combination of regular expressions and manual validation of a sample subset. The dataset reflects the limitations of the courts' digitization practices discussed above. Missing metadata fields, non-standardized case numbering, and inconsistent formatting constrain certain types of analysis.

Our scraping captured judgments as they appeared at the time of collection (February 2023 to April 2023); subsequent corrections or amendments to published judgments would not be reflected in the dataset. Although we have attempted to address several of these challenges in our present work, the findings therefore may remain affected by their residuary impact and other unknown issues.

## Dataset Scope and Coverage

The corpus captures only those judgments that the courts chose to publish on their official websites; unpublished judgments, orders, and procedural decisions remain beyond our scope. The empirical data is the data available on the official websites of the Supreme Court and Court of Appeal. The paper relies on the official websites for the coverage of these judgments. However, courts issue many different orders, including interlocutory orders, interim relief, enjoining orders, and final judgments. To get a full picture, one would need to look at all of these, but our empirical study only looks at judgments.

Furthermore, there is an inconsistency in how the courts use "orders" versus "judgments". The Court of Appeal website currently has separate sections for judgments and orders, and often final judgments are published under the "Orders" section. This means that a portion of the court's output might be missed if only the "judgments" section is scraped.

In the case of writs and fundamental rights petitions, proceedings are in two stages. When a petition has been filed, the Court initially conducts a hearing to determine whether leave to proceed will be granted. At this stage, it is possible for a petition to be dismissed. However, whether or not a judgment or order is issued to record a dismissal, is at the discretion of the Courts. Where leave to proceed is granted, Court may also grant interim relief sought by the petitioner. In such instances, Court issues an order.

The SriLankaCourtDecisions corpus does not include data on dismissals at the leave to proceed stage as that data is not produced as a written record. To that extent the data on dismissal have a significant limitation as it does not depict the accurate number of dismissals by courts. The actual dismissal rates are therefore higher than the data suggests, since many petitions are dismissed at the leave stage without a written judgment being issued. This limitation affects our analysis of outcomes in [Section 4](#sec:insights).

## Implications

Despite these limitations, the SriLankaCourtDecisions corpus represents the most comprehensive corpus of Sri Lankan appellate judgments assembled to date and provides sufficient coverage for meaningful empirical analysis of recent trends in the Superior Courts. The findings are preliminary but sufficient to challenge received assumptions and warrant attention in reform debates. The necessity of interpreting the empirical alongside the normative and doctrinal, and not in isolation, only increases in importance during the formulation of any such reform.
